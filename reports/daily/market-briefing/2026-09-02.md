## Key Drivers

- **GR_WEST midday lull at 1,108 MW (HE14) is the sharpest single deterioration vs Sep 1**: The West Texas anticyclone is no longer moderating — GR_WEST has now compressed to 1.1 GW during the peak demand window, approaching the worst suppression days of the heat wave core (Days 7-10 floors were 863-2,572 MW). This leaves essentially zero West Texas wind contribution during the critical HE13-17 corridor when ERCOT load is 77-84 GW. The thermal stack must compensate for roughly 1,880 MW more of West Texas wind absence vs Sep 1, which directly elevates HE13-18 pricing. Congestion on West Texas transmission pathways may be less of a concern today given the near-zero available flow, but the absence of generation is the primary issue.

- **Thermal outages up ~817 MW average vs Sep 1 (+1,000-1,200 MW in the afternoon peak window)**: The elevated cap-out during the critical HE13-18 window (9,239-9,240 MW vs Sep 1's ~8,000-8,539 MW) is concerning. This likely reflects additional scheduled maintenance filings taking advantage of the high-solar midday window — a rational decision given 22-27 GW of solar that displaces thermal — but the consequence is that the available thermal stack enters the HE17-18 plateau period with 1,000+ MW less headroom than Sep 1. The HE18→HE19 step-down (8,939 → 8,775 MW) indicates some units are returning, but the evening floor of 8,775 MW remains 850-930 MW above Sep 1's 7,845-7,925 MW floor.

- **Net load at HE20 nearly identical to Sep 1 (64,215 vs 64,515 MW) despite lower gross load**: This structural convergence confirms that the Sep 2 evening cliff is equally stressed as yesterday's — the 1,368 MW reduction in gross load is almost exactly offset by 866 MW less wind at HE20 and 9,072 MW solar cliff (vs Sep 1's 10,182 MW cliff; the smaller solar volume today means less cliff magnitude, but solar reaches a lower absolute value, 2,300 MW vs Sep 1's 2,576 MW). The thermal dispatch requirement at HE20 converges to 64,864 MW (Sep 1: 65,576 MW) — essentially the same thermal call on back-to-back days.

- **GR_COASTAL sea breeze (HE19 = 3,735 MW) provides 594 MW more support than Sep 1 at the exact cliff onset**: The coastal sea breeze peaks at precisely HE19, the first hour of the steep cliff. With GR_COASTAL at 3,735 MW on Sep 2 vs 3,141 MW on Sep 1, this provides meaningful incremental cover at the onset of the hardest transition. The coastal contribution remains solid through HE20 (3,520 MW) and HE21 (3,174 MW), partially offsetting the weaker GR_WEST and weaker total ERCOT wind vs Sep 1. This is the key structural positive differentiator for Sep 2.

- **Solar is weaker (-3,715 MW peak) but the net effect is a less extreme duck curve**: The Sep 2 midday net load trough (35,032 MW at HE10) is substantially less depressed than Sep 1's record-level 32,127 MW trough. This means Sep 2 avoids the deep midday price suppression of Sep 1 (where prices approached $10-25/MWh) and instead sees $20-35/MWh in the solar belly — more normal for a hot summer weekday. The tradeoff is a shallower solar cliff magnitude at HE19→HE20 (-9,072 MW vs Sep 1's -10,182 MW), which reduces the single-hour ramp demand on thermal. However, the absolute wind level at HE20 is also weaker, leaving net stress nearly unchanged.

- **Heat wave Day 13 load moderating but still significantly above climatological baseline**: The 84,500 MW Wednesday peak is approximately 3-5 GW above a typical September Wednesday based on historical September load patterns (typical Sep Wed peaks ~79-82 GW). The residual heat stored in building thermal mass from 13 consecutive days of above-90°F temperatures sustains elevated baseline cooling load even as peak temperatures begin to moderate. The 58,821 MW overnight trough (HE5) reflects continued elevated minimum temperatures — overnight low temperatures have been 5-8°F above normal throughout the heat wave.
